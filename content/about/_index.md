## Who am I?

I'm André Mota and I'm a composer turned software engineer from Braga, in Portugal. Music is always a part of my life, but it has been mostly a hobby these past few years. Don't be fooled, however, I love being a software engineer.

Today, I work as a cloud-native backend developer who daily drives NixOS and runs a homelab. The path that brought me here was, to say the least, peculiar. If you came for tech content, you came to the right place. You don't need sharp trigonometry skills, but I can't promise I won't send you on a few tangents.

**TL;DR**

- 2011&ndash;2016: Wanting to play Doom II sends me on a journey of understanding 16-bit software on 64-bit OSs, learning virtual machines, working with 90s hardware, and bricking said hardware.
- 2016&ndash;2020: I start a composition degree in Lisbon, work with real-time audio processing in Max/MSP, and stumble into functional programming via OpenMusic.
- 2020&ndash;Present: An involuntary gap year leads to a CS degree (and Haskell, C, compilers...), a simple Minecraft server turns into a full homelab, and I relocate to Germany.

## How It Began

I developed an interest in music from a very young age, and this was an interest that I have since cherished for most of my life. Alongside this interest, however, and probably influenced by always having had a computer at home, these always caught my attention.

My biggest interest in computers, however, wouldn't appear until I decided that I wanted to play [Doom II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doom_(franchise)). The Doom franchise is known for its technological advancements, which are almost as impressive as John Romero's hair. At this point in time, however, I was more interested in playing the game that seemingly made my parents want to have a computer back in 1996.

I, with my naïvety, decided that the contender for playing Doom II would be my personal machine. A rather new 2010 Toshiba Satellite Pro C650, my first own computer. This machine had a modest Intel Core 2 Duo with only its integrated graphics and 4GB of the brand new DDR3 memory, clocked at a whopping 1066MHz (believe it or not, this machine was only retired in 2023). This machine also featured the 1 year old Windows 7 Professional (_Satellite **Pro**_), just around the end of 32-bit operating systems. I'm pretty sure that you, the most attentive reader, might see exactly where this is going right now. To you, and you know exactly who you are, you already saw it coming when I said the computer had 4GB of RAM. This is exactly the time that I got my hands on the original game executable, and, as excited as I was, I got the dead-end pop-up that I'm pretty sure crushed hopes, dreams, and probably businesses: `Unsupported 16-Bit Application`.

## Down The Rabbit Hole(s)

A bit of contemporary trivia, probably for you, the younger reader. Windows XP was the greatest invention since sliced bread and Windows Vista needed a NASA grade computer to run compared to it. This is all you need to know. This means that when Windows 7 came out, asking for even higher computer specifications than Vista, mind you, everyone and their dog was on Windows XP. Windows XP's main versions were 32-bit, and this meant that backwards compatibility to the 16-bit instruction set was possible. On top of that, the _not MS-DOS command line_ of Windows XP was actually quite usable as a DOS replacement. That pop-up I got wasn't just a pebble on the road for me, but rather the end of an era.

With this said, how did I solve my problem? Because I pretty much tried everything but the sane options, here is a best of:

- Windows 7 32-bit version: you'd think this one would solve the problem. So did I. Except that right after running the setup program, it crashed because Windows 7 didn't support going fullscreen on the command line.
- [Windows XP Mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_PC#Windows_XP_Mode): amazing work Microsoft did with this one (_in the age of Copilot-everything, this sounds unbelievable_). This was a fully integrated virtualized environment (a [WSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Subsystem_for_Linux) on steroids &mdash; no, really) that Microsoft came up with to bring back some of that magical backwards compatibility Windows XP had with the MS-DOS age. Doom II and its specific hardware assumptions made it basically impossible to run properly, even when I went the route of opening the entire VM, however.
- Windows 95 VM: read above. Exact same issue, with the added _bonus_ of shoving an imperfect 32-bit architecture with questionable Virtual PC 2007 drivers into a 64-bit operating system.
- Windows 95 VM on VirtualBox: the outcome was not that different.

Here's the solution I came up with: real hardware. This is the early 2010s, so hardware from the 90s isn't that easy to come by. I.e. if you don't already own it. If you remember correctly, I mentioned that the Toshiba was my first own computer. That's half of the truth. The Toshiba was indeed my first computer that didn't also double as a paperweight.

Enter my [Compaq Armada 1500 something series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compaq_Armada), with a Pentium MMX, 32MB of RAM, and a 3D sound that made [Windows 98 truly sound like a spaceship](https://youtu.be/tajDxBaPBBM). It's perfect. With its fast 20x CD-R drive (the Toshiba, a computer that was probably 15 years newer, had a combo drive that was only capable of 24x), absolutely no internet connection, nor USB, this was the _perfect_ contender. Well, not exactly... But it did the job.

## Tinkering Is A One Way Trip

After picking up this Windows 98 laptop, I needed proper time accurate hardware. Time to get the old monitor from that old 1996 computer, its keyboard, its mice, and its speakers. And so I used the computer like that. I actually finished Doom II on that computer.

However, this wasn't enough (_famous last words..._). The CD-R drive, the only way I had to transfer data to this computer, was old and tired. Either that or those 20x looked better on paper than in reality. Also, I found out that laptops from the 90s, even when their specifications looked really nice on paper, weren't the most reliable things, even to play the epitome of _runs everywhere_ that Doom is. It was time for an upgrade.

That old museum piece from the end of 1996 that used to be the family computer (contextualizing this to you, the younger reader, once upon a time, when a house had a computer, it was usually just one), was in the basement of the house, gaining dust (and probably a good dose of humidity). When I say museum piece I mean it, since this was one of those old computer towers, the ones with a turbo button. It was time to turn it on. With one hand close to the button of the computer and another one ready to pull the plug (_...in case of emergencies_), I turned it on. This computer had been 7 years without being turned on. There it was. The first computer I ever touched. After a familiar cloud picture, with some intermissions in the form of `AUTOEXEC.BAT`, a familiar spaceship sound again. And, to my surprise, the user name was my name. This machine, ever since it had been replaced by the Windows XP computer at home, was actually meant for me.

The beans in the machine: the CMOS battery was gone. This would be its state until its retirement. The computer had an original Intel Pentium clocked at 133MHz, 16MB of RAM, a very modest S3 Trio 32 graphics card, and a [cog rattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratchet_(instrument)) that doubled as a Seagate HDD with 1275MB. This was the one that started an interest in computer hardware that exists until today.

Working with this particular computer was an even bigger challenge. Things we take for granted like [Plug and Play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plug_and_play), USB, or even a connection to the internet weren't a thing here, as well. The computer had a very handy 8x CD-R drive. Imagine, a whole _8x_ instead of _4x_! But fear not, I already had burned CDs for the other computer! And this was the moment I learned why [disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling_of_disc) drives have a very visible speed specification. This meant spending a lot of (additional) time burning CDs with whichever files I needed to pass to that computer, hoping I had downloaded the right driver for whatever [Sound Blaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_Blaster) compatible [ISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_Standard_Architecture) sound card it had. Because I wanted this machine back to its original state, as well, I formatted it with Windows 95.

I interrupt your reading now for "A Lesson In Formatting Original 90s Hardware" (_especially if it sounds like [Depeche Mode's People Are People](https://youtu.be/MzGnX-MbYE4)_): it's a day task, one that has some very convenient pauses. Start by enjoying your lunch while you have your drive being formatted with an MS-DOS bootdisk, with enough time to spare to have a coffee outside. Come back just in time to start the timezone and other personal setup, and leave the computer do its thing when copying files. By the time you finish dinner, the computer will be ready.

And this was the moment I noticed I was already deep into the rabbit hole. At this point in time, I have this particular machine that could run Doom II, the Compaq Armada with Windows 98 equipped with some Intel Pentium MMX and 32MB of RAM that could also run Doom II, and an ever growing pile of CD-R with the most varied driver installers, Windows patches, and, most importantly, Doom, Doom II, FullTilt Pinball (if you remember Pinball coming with Windows, this is the full game), etc. Parallel to this, I still have my Toshiba, with Windows 7 and a dual-boot with Ubuntu, even though I was a Windows ricer at the time. One of my favourite rices was to make Windows 7 look exactly like Windows 98, even.

## University Changes A Lot

Some time passes, I stop using the 2 old machines and retire them, I find [zdoom](https://zdoom.org/index) and [DosBox](https://www.dosbox.com/index.php). By this time I had also changed my main machine to a desktop equipped with an Intel Core i5 3570K, 16GB of RAM, and an Nvidia GTX 650ti. I also find out about this _new_ thing called SSD. This machine wouldn't stay with me that long, so I upgraded, more or less 2.5 years later to an Intel i7 4790K,16GB of RAM, and an Nvidia GTX 970. This machine was the one I kept for the longest time as my main machine.

When the time to decide to go to university comes, even after pursuing music in high school, I'm faced with the choice of pursuing software engineering or continuing with composition, which had been my actual area of studies. I choose the latter, obviously, and on I go to live away from my parents for the first time. This was by the time I was on my second desktop computer, which came with me to Lisbon, and I got a new laptop (an Asus GL502VT with an Intel Core i7 6700HQ, 16GB of DDR4 RAM (my first DDR4 machine), and an Nvidia GTX 970m&mdash;_foreshadowing_), and the tinkering continues. This particular desktop had, at one point, Ubuntu, Mac OS 10.13, and Windows 10 installed at the same time.

It would be here, however, that my biggest calling to software engineering yet would appear. In the subjects related to electronic music, I learned tools like [PureData](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Data) and [CSound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Csound) (I didn't like this one very much, and yes, I understand the irony), but my passion (and time sink, let's be real) would come in the form of [Max/MSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_(software)) and [OpenMusic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenMusic).

Max was described by most of my peers as a demonic spaghetti that functioned pretty much like a black box with a mind of its own. Max for me was tinkering meets creative freedom to the maximum. If it could be represented by sound, then it was possible in Max. With my current knowledge, I can say that Max was a realtime async sound engine with a just slightly simplified interface. Max has a very clever design, directly separating sound, messages, triggers, and, very importantly, memory. It has it all. I was able to make several FM and additive synthesizers. My proudest patch, however, was one I titled PatchHellBell (a pun between [Pachelbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Pachelbel) and AC/DC's Hell's Bells), which had various iterations. This patch began as a media player that could play at any given velocity and in both directions, which could be changed in real time. One day, using the UI of this patch, I added a parametrized concatenative synthesis section to the patch, which implied getting timing correct to the sample within the patch. I think, to this day, I was never able to mentally divide by 44100 as fast as I was during that time.

My biggest time sink was OpenMusic. This one, at the time, felt different. Things happened almost magically. Logic just made sense. It was almost as if I could program things that I didn't necessarily write. And this was my first impression of functional programming, without knowing. With OpenMusic I made several patches that would construct scores to help voicing chords. This also coincided with the time that I was playing a lot with the harmonic series, which means that I programmed the patch to be able to voice, on demand, using a number of chosen [fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_frequency). This would then create textures with the number of voices I requested and help me have an idea of different voicings for different spectra. I found it quite funny and engaging at the time. Today, I would like to know why I was programming decisions trees using spaghetti (all these graphical user interfaces looked like spaghetti) in music. Either way, I found something I liked. One curiosity: did you know that every partial that is a prime number is a new note?

## The Pandemic

By the time I finished the course, I was lucky enough to be starting making a name for myself in Lisbon's music scene, both as a performer, and, ultimately, as a composer. Things were looking very bright and I was confident that everything would turn out for the better. I had projects incoming and I had great plans of studying in the Netherlands for a master's. This was until the pandemic hit. This changed everything. I stopped having gigs altogether, and my plans and commissions ended up never happening. Without concerts there's no commissions, and without commissions I don't have a career in composition. To top it off, I wasn't accepted in the master's in the Netherlands, after interviewing, on the grounds of being too young and to enjoy life.

Being quite fair, that's what I did. With the gap year I didn't plan, I thought this was the perfect time to continue tinkering with making electronic music. Which obviously means that I got myself yet another old computer. I promise this makes sense.

My only acousmatic piece ever, which I entitled _Obsoleto_ (meaning obsolete in English), used artifacts of sound from olden times, such as the TX-16 sampler and some dated keyboard sounds. The whole concept of the piece was the usage of sounds that could somehow remind of other times. I even incorporated some of the Pinball sounds and I used a door sound as a homage of [Pierre Henry's Variations For a Door and a Sigh](https://pierre-henry.org/items/show/1693). The _piece de resistance_, however, which would become a foundation for a sound I could never reach, was a [dial-up modem](https://youtu.be/vvr9AMWEU-c). You can already see it coming: I decided to try to do it the hard way. If it exists in sound form, Max can do it, right? But where's the fun in that?

To this day, I haven't figured out the modem situation yet. I know, a let down. When it happens it will end up in this blog. I have a time accurate computer, I have the modem, so... Where's the problem?

Here's my setup: if I can have a server and a telephone relay, I can 100% reproduce modem sounds at will. In this setup, my modern machine controls the whole connection. The old computer running things in real hardware won't know it's talking to a _fake modem_. Some trivia and a bit of the theory behind this. Because modems ran on landlines, and landlines were made for phone signal, not internet signal, the frequency range supported by these lines resides in the audible spectrum. After all, why spend more bandwidth if it would otherwise be useless? Enter modems. Modems, upon connection, need to make a handshake. Preferrably, one that can be diagnosed by the modem to detect analog signal degradation. All of this happens in the audible spectrum, and this is precisely the part I wanted to exploit. I completely lacked the knowledge to do so, and, I believe that today I also do. I think today I know where to start looking, at least.

Enough about modems, what about the computer around it? It's yet another white box, this time slightly newer, from maybe the year 2000. This one I picked up from a random pile from an IT store and hoped for the best. I have to admit that the choosing method was based on "looks 90s enough to me." The hardware had to get me the best of a nice retro gaming machine with time-accurate hardware, and hopefully a nice compatibility with dial-up modems. So, the lottery gave me a very leaky (_yet another thing I need to fix, these machines from the capacitor-gate era have a very particular smell to them_) but functional machine with an AMD Duron clocked at 800MHz, an amount of RAM I increased to, I believe, 384MB, a disk with some respectable 20GB, and I added some things from a hardware box lottery, same provenance.

Inspecting upon Schrödinger's box _rendered_ me an AGP 4x Nvidia TNT Riva 2, perfectly compatible with this piece of machinery. PCIe would still need some more years to come. Finally, my personal favourite: a Creative Sound Blaster Live 5.1 that sounds better than any integrated soundcard any modern computer has.

This poor machine had Windows XP pre-installed, which seemed to be Service Pack 3. With SP3 applied, this was Windows XP and wasn't. Because this machine barely met the minimum requirements for base Windows XP, let alone end-of-life Windows XP (_the fact that we are looking at a machine I believe is from the year 2000 running Windows XP just goes to show how ubiquitous it was_), Windows XP had to leave. And don't even get me started with Windows ME... This machine was going to have Windows 98 installed. This time, lucky me, I had USB. The most unusable and unreliable possible USB ports ever (any relation leaking capacitors is a mere coincidence...). The times that the USB ports worked, they were actually able to let me copy a good amount of games and, for the first time ever, Windows 98 patches. This computer was able to play Sega Rally Championship, Sega Rally Championship 2, some early Need for Speed titles, and some more titles from that era. I would say it isn't time accurate, but more that it would be an accurate upgraded machine.

All this excitement with this machine, and some questionable hardware montages later to have it working (_as much as the capacitors allowed_) I decided it was time to make an important decision. Out of gigs and out of master's, I thought that it was time to put my plan B in action.

## University, pt. II

When going to music, which is a career notorious for being prolific in unemployment, I decided to still grant myself a spot in the software engineering course. This was my plan B. There's no time like the now, people say, and I decided that better than a master's only 2 bachelor's. So I went.

I've been talking (_ranting, even!_) about previous endeavours, _encounters_ if I may, between hardware and software. I never hid opinions I had about things throughout when I believe they shaped my general interest in tech. This to say: I have quite of a strong opinion about this specific course I attended. I'm pretty sure parts of it apply because of my specific vocational transition and profile, which, as you can see, until this moment has been very practice focused.

I went to Universidade do Minho, after a forced less than ideal gap year and spending 3 years studying in a bigger city in a completely different area of studies. To say the shock was big is an understatement. The teaching methodologies and approach to teaching were quite different, as well. I was used to classes where the biggest, by far, had between 40-50 people. Here, classes were small if they had that number. The version of an algorithms subject this particular course had was notorious for having around 650 people attending every year, with 2 sessions of the lecture classes every week, just to be able to accommodate the sheer number. These classes would routinely have around 200 people attending. But I was missing the hands on approach I loved in most of these subjects. So, my tactic became quite simple: extract the good parts and forget about the bad. The bad is just a necessary part needed to be able to enjoy the good. And this was my approach to everything I learned throughout this course.

Having this being my approach, there were interesting subjects I thought were bad, there were uninteresting subjects I thought were good. This was a general feeling that manifested in different ways for a lot of people. For me, it manifested in noticing that I was learning more because I was seeing the theory behind things I've seen before, and also because the practice assignments, far too many times, required knowledge that went beyond what was taught in classes. This is expected, I just didn't expect it to apply to entire chapters of knowledge here. Allow me to present a best of what I liked in the course and cherish as being things I learned there that help me to this day:

- Haskell: I love this programming language and I would admit that this is the mental model behind how I program everything. It also explained why OpenMusic felt different. I learned this language through the lens of a Functional Programming syllabus, a practical assignment that ironically shows how not to use it, and a wonderful Programming Calculus subject which, to me, is one of the best I've had in the whole course. This last one is the total foundation of how I think about data flow nowadays.

- C: To this day, the programming language I consider I know the best. Every semester of the 6 in total had 6 subjects. Sometimes theory, sometimes practical, sometimes both. C was present in 5 of the 36 in total, being the most present programming language out of any of them. I saw this language being used in a way I don't think it should be used in an assignment, in operating systems, and, for a simple parser for a language using Hungarian notation. This last one became probably my most giant rabbit hole in the whole of programming.

- Compilers: This one is a bit tricky. Supposedly, I learned about them in a subject from the very last semester called "Languages Processing". And yet, the knowledge about compilers themselves came from a bunch more places. It came from algorithms, definitely, from computer architecture, a subject that cemented things I seemingly already knew in processor instructions, a lot about just talking about C's memory model with older colleagues, and, surprisingly enough, program calculus. 

- Networks: This is actually a big one. If you read up to this point, you might notice I never mentioned networks. And the reason is simple: networks were this thing that existed and made absolutely no sense to me. This is the first time that signal processing, something I kind of knew, async programming, something I apparently kind of knew as well, and applying it to communicating with other computers made sense. The model is quite simple, actually, this was just the first time I noticed. I wouldn't notice exactly how simple the model is, however, until after this course.

These were the biggest 4 things the course taught me. _There's no software theory in any of those, how come that is?_, you might be asking. And that's exactly my biggest critique. Software theory and design in the syllabus of the course, as it was when I attended it, came late within the syllabus, needing an update to modern standards, and overall less strictness, to come close to the software industry standards. I understand that the strictness comes with how subjects are usually evaluated. This, to me, would be a reason to lean on making these practice subjects. Software theory and design is about putting together a software pipeline that can produce results and the capacity of a development team to apply it. This was something that I can confidently say I only even understood, not just learned, after working in a team at a company. Therefore, despite being important, this was something I wouldn't say I learned at the course.

I do consider that the basis of the course were quite nice, and I had a lot of fun doing some of the practical assignments. I have to admit, most seem more fun in hindsight than they did at the time. I lost the count as to how many practical assignments I had throughout the course. Officially, there were 4 dedicated practice subjects, but from the 3rd semester onwards, every subject that mixed theory and practice would have a practical assignment. This brings the number closer to 20 in total. Speaking about each and everyone of them would be an exercise in repetition, so I'll mention: one that taught me a lot involved making a DNS system from scratch, but the one I cherish the most was my languages processing assignnment. This was about making a functional extension to Python using [PLY](https://ply.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html), and I dedicated so much time to it I called it `fpy`. I'd like to revisit `fpy`, in fact. I wouldn't do it justice if I talked about it here, so, one day, expect a dedicated post.

Of course, I learned a lot in this university, if not by anything else, because of everything I had to cram in to finish all the assignments. This was also the beginning and the establishment of my journey with Linux as more of a main operating system, which would become a one way road.

## Sometimes You Go 2 Steps Back...

After university, I decided to go on a gap year. Premeditated, this time around. This had me ending up in Germany working for a non-profit serving as somewhat of an artistic director. This was the perfect time to finally start the implementation of a project that I was invited to do.

Oh, right, I haven't mentioned this. That's because this is where it becomes important! During my last semester in university, I was given an opportunity to implement a tool that could help making song chord sheets more easily. Given I have knowledge of both causes, this is actually the kind of project that, albeit more focused in software, could perfectly join both my areas of expertise. As such, I joined this project. This is a classic case of _"we have no process, please, pretty please come up with one."_ The original process was, quite literally, searching a computer's filesystem (_I believe this piece of machinery didn't have an SSD, even_) for the files in question. So, this is the kind of _no process_ we're dealing with. It's no wonder it took me this long, also because I couldn't work on this even close to full time, that no implementation had been done. For this, I conducted interviews and I asked a few questions to the future users of the system. When I had enough information, I started implementation.

If you got this far, congratulations, this is the first time I'm going to speak about implementing software! Since I had total freedom (_how come I wouldn't, I was the only developer and there was literally nothing_), I chose the tooling, bearing in mind as much future proofing as I could, and that I would be the first to implement, but not the last owner of the codebase. So, if this were for me, I would've chosen Rust + SvelteKit. I wanted to learn Rust, and I wanted to learn SvelteKit. But I actually didn't have any web development experience. Right... So, the basis of knowledge given to me was good, remember? I didn't have a single subject teaching web development. `HTTP` was briefly mentioned in a subject appropriately named "Computer Communications", where I learned more about DNS than I thought possible.

The verdict: Go + React. I don't think I need to explain why React (_this will become a "why never React" in the future_), but why Go? I refused to use Node.js for the backend. As someone who had a really good theory understanding at this point and 0 enterprise knowledge, I actually thought Go was an upcoming language in web-dev circles. I like how I wasn't wrong, but I wasn't right either. In retrospective, the reason "because Go is an upcoming language that I think will take Java's usage in backend" was a bit too optimistic. Nowadays, I still stand by my choice, but for different reasons. But I would never use "this is the first and last time that _I_ touch this codebase, but it still needs maintaining" as the argument to choose Go as a widely available language. Being quite fair, Java has probably more contenders.

With this out of the way, I started implementing the application. I knew databases, I actually had a quite sane and complex databases subject in university, and I can guarantee that this made half of the data modelling quite better. I had never touched TypeScript nor Go. Time to learn! So, for TypeScript, I decided to look at some things and talk to a friend who is a frontend developer. For Go I decided to redo a whole assignment from my university, but instead of using C, I used Go. I'm 100% sure it had logic errors, but I loved it. I even asked myself "why didn't I use this for the DNS assignment?" (it was Python, if you're wondering...).

So, I was able to eventually have an application that worked with a horrible, horrible frontend, and I was happy. Time to try to deploy it. There's no time like today, and learning how to deploy applications was actually something I inadvertently learned earlier. Going to Germany meant that I would lose access to a bunch of things I had back home, starting with my desktop computer, which this time was a fantastic machine with an AMD Ryzen 9 5900X, 32GB of RAM clocked at 3600MHz (very important for Ryzen), and an Nvidia RTX 3070. This computer stayed in Portugal while I was in Germany, and ever since I started to have desktop computers (_can you believe it, it had been more than 10 years since the first one!_) I always wanted to have access to at least one. Going to Germany I would have 0 access to my desktop, but I decided that I would bring a bit of home with me...

Enter my homelab. This will sound repetitive, but I entered this niche the way I enter pretty much everything: I wanted to play a game in a certain way. This time the game is Minecraft. I didn't mention this before, but alongside my Doom II endeavour, I also played other games. Counter-Strike 1.6, Need for Speed (some titles, some times), Crysis, etc. But there was one game that took more time than any other: _Minecraft_. With my newly acquired networks knowledge, and some newly acquired hardware in a familiar package (the Asus Maximus VII Hero I had on my previous desktop decided to ignite one of its VRM 2 years prior to this... I had made it my family computer and it simply decided it had enough), I decided it was time to do some Proxmox magic, and suddenly I had a Minecraft server. I bought my first domain, and _boom_, there it was: my whitelisted Minecraft server where I could play with other people. This machine also doubled as a fantastic NAS, with the most brittle setup ever (I will definitely post when I upgrade), and, very importantly, my personal VPN. This tangent will make sense. Both for this part of my life, but also for the future content of my blog.

Considering that I was spending a lot of time programming and tinkering with software, this was the time that I looked at my Arch Linux installation, which at the time was using XMonad and was my main development environment, and I decided "let's switch things up a bit." I had heard of this _new-fangled_ thing (not that new...) called NixOS. This was the first time I tried it. Because I couldn't afford to lose my main development machine, which at the time was a Lenovo ThinkPad T460 with 16GB of RAM and an Intel Core i5 6200, I went to my _main machine_, the now 7 years old Asus. So, I am developing a web-app with Go + React, maintaining a [Proxmox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxmox_Virtual_Environment) homelab, and now trying to get [NixOS](https://wiki.nixos.org/wiki/NixOS_Wiki) to work on a machine that has 2 GPU.

Those of you familiar with this Strix line of computers have probably started laughing at the screen the moment I mentioned this would be the contender.
